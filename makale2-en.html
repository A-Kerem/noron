Quantum Computing: Are We Really Ready?

April 28, 2026
5 min read
Noron Editorial

Announcements from Google and IBM in recent years have sent waves of excitement through the quantum computing world. "Quantum supremacy" was declared, records were broken, billions of dollars were invested. But the core question remains unanswered: Will this technology truly enter everyday life, or is it still too far away?

Quantum computers can solve certain problems quadrillions of times faster than classical computers — but only those specific problems.

What Is Quantum Computing and Why Does It Matter?

Classical computers process everything with 0s and 1s — bits. Quantum computers use units called qubits. A qubit can be both 0 and 1 simultaneously; this is called superposition. This property allows certain calculations to be performed at incredible speed.

Potential application areas of quantum computing

  • Drug discovery — Designing new medicines via molecular simulations
  • Cryptography — Breaking existing encryption and developing new methods
  • Artificial intelligence — Training machine learning models much faster
  • Logistics — Complex route and supply chain optimization
  • Finance — Risk analysis and portfolio optimization

What Did Google and IBM Say?

In 2019, Google declared "quantum supremacy" with its 53-qubit Sycamore processor, claiming to solve a problem in 200 seconds that would take classical computers 10,000 years. IBM immediately challenged this claim. By 2025, IBM announced significant steps toward fault-tolerant quantum computing.

The Real Problem: Error and Noise

Qubits are incredibly unstable. Temperature, vibration, electromagnetic interference — all can corrupt a qubit. This is why quantum computers typically operate near absolute zero (-273°C). Researchers use "quantum error correction" to address this, but it requires many extra qubits.

When Will It Be Ready?

Expert estimates vary widely. Optimistic scenarios suggest real-world applications will be possible by the early 2030s. More cautious views predict at least 10-20 more years. One thing is certain: Quantum computing will not replace classical computers — they will complement each other.

Conclusion: The Excitement Is Justified, But Patience Is Required

Google and IBM's announcements reflect genuine scientific progress. But some of these headlines also carry a marketing dimension. Quantum computing is real and important — but the moment that "changes everything" hasn't arrived yet.
